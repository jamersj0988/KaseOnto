Kauklahti Senior Centre
Espoo, Finland
Kauklahti Living and Dwelling Centre for the Elderly is the first built example of a new service-home concept. The centre offers local senior citizens a public living room with various services; meals, exercise and pastime activities. The dwellings are for the elderly who need 24/7 care. The environment is designed to be easy to use in order to encourage residents’ autonomy. The rooms look like homes despite the many assistive devices.

The dwellings are clustered around a light-filled and spacious living room with a balcony and a kitchen. The corridors are minimized to make both physical and social accessibility easy for the residents. The ground floor contains the service centre; a restaurant, a gym with a sauna and communal rooms for recreational activities, as well as a winter garden.

The cluster concept assists in situating this large building into the small scale of the neighbourhood and the rocky terrain. The building consists of three cubic volumes and a connecting circulation joint with the main staircase and lifts. The colours refer to the colours of the surrounding housing.

The uneven terrain was demanding, however, it provided opportunities for landscaping. Due to the slope, residents have access to courtyards from different floors of the building and semi-underground spaces get natural lighting.

The project contains various advanced techniques; for the residents as well as for the building. For example, rooms have a smart floor system for unobtrusive monitoring, security checks and alerts. The energy solutions are based on using geothermal energy in an innovative way. The building obtains 50% of its energy for heating and cooling from a renewable source, resulting in low carbon emissions. Making use of innovative, affordable, and efficient structural solutions also contribute to reducing maintenance costs.

II. Spatial Relationship Descriptions
- The plan is organized as three distinct wings (clusters of units) that radiate from and connect to a central core — the elevator/stair cluster — via short corridors and a linking walkway.
- Units are arranged along the perimeter of each wing, with corridors running down the wing centers; this places private rooms to the outside for daylight and ventilation while corridors form a semi-public interior spine.
- Each wing contains a central, slightly larger common/living zone flanked by repetitive unit modules, so individual units cluster around a localized communal space within the wing.
- Vertical circulation is concentrated at the central hub and supplemented by secondary stair locations within the wings, making circulation hierarchical: central core (public/vertical) → wing corridor (semi-public) → apartment entrance (private).
- Balconies and terraces are positioned on the outer faces of wings, linking private units to exterior space and visually breaking the building mass; the bridging walkway creates explicit physical and visual connections between otherwise separated volumes.
- Service and utility shafts are clustered adjacent to the cores and unit bathrooms, allowing shared plumbing/MEP runs and efficient servicing across repeated units.

III. Overall Spatial Impression (1–2 sentences)
The drawing shows a hub-and-spoke residential layout with three angled wings of repeated apartment modules radiating from a centralized vertical circulation core, producing distinct clusters that balance privacy (perimeter-facing units) with connectivity (central elevators/stairs and linking bridge). The plan emphasizes modular repetition, perimeter daylighting, and a clear circulation hierarchy from public core to private units.

IV. Design Intent Reading
- Efficiency and repeatability: the repeated unit modules and clustered service shafts indicate an intention for efficient construction, standardized layouts, and economical servicing (plumbing/electrical).
- Privacy and daylighting: placing units on the perimeter with corridors internal suggests an intent to maximize natural light and external views for living/bedroom spaces while keeping circulation compact.
- Community + connectivity: central core and intermediate communal spaces inside each wing, plus the linking walkway, imply an aim to foster social interaction among residents while maintaining discrete household domains.
- Spatial articulation and site response: the angled wings and inserted terraces create small semi-exterior pockets and varied sightlines — likely intended to improve privacy between units, capture specific orientations/views, and break down the building mass.      
- Clear hierarchy of movement: concentration of vertical circulation in a central hub with secondary wing stairs shows a deliberate organization to prioritize accessibility and wayfinding.
