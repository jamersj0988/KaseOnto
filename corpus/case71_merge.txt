Le Village Landais Alzheimer

Location: Dax, France
Architects: NORD Architects in collaboration with Champagnat & Gregoire Architects
Client: Département des Landes
Year: 1st prize competition 2016. Inaugurated in phases. In summer 2020, the first residents moved in.
Size: 10,700 m2
Construction costs: EUR 20 million


The number of people with dementia is on the rise in both Denmark and Europe. We are facing a welfare challenge that is going to develop, as the global number of elderly people increases. This will add great pressure on the entire health care sector, in terms of capacity, physical environment, treatment and care. So how do we rise to the challenge and create built environments to accommodate the significant trends we are currently witnessing, while providing space for efficient, soothing, healing treatment?

NORD Architects have designed several dementia villages, including the Alzheimer’s Village in Dax, which is the first care home in France for people with dementia. The architects have taken into account the individual residents, the health care staff, and the local culture and nature, so everyone, from relatives to researchers, will experience people – including those with dementia – living in an environment that prioritises dignified aging.
Alzheimer’s Village in Dax is designed to create an environment, in which residents, relatives and health care professionals all get a feeling of well-being, which is also a major prerequisite for providing qualified nursing and care. There is currently no medical cure for dementia, but research indicates that several elements in the environment can have a positive effect on symptoms. Being able to recognise one’s surroundings is essential for leading a meaningful life in an environment that’s seems like a natural continuation of the former home and neighborhood – free from alienating or obstructive elements. Alzheimer’s Village has integrated familiar functions within the centre – a grocer’s, a hairdresser’s, a restaurant and a market square – reminiscent of the residents’ previous lives in their neighbourhoods. This helps provide a sense of a relatively normal, recognisable day-to-day life. The design was inspired by the local architectural style and tradition and is adapted to the site, featuring recognisable architecture to make residents feel at home and give them a natural sense of belonging.
Morten Gregersen, architect and partner of NORD Architects, describes the need for recognisability in everyday life and architecture, as follows: “For people with Alzheimer’s or dementia, it is crucial that the environment is recognisable. It mustn’t feel challenging or disturb their cognitive abilities. That’s why this architecture also has an explicitly local feel, featuring elements from the local building style. The built environment provides a kind of cultural extension that alleviates the transition from living at home to living with a severe mental illness in an Alzheimer’s centre.”
The functions such as hairdresser’s, market square and restaurant are also open to the neighbouring community, so the residents of Alzheimer’s Village can enjoy contact with the locals. The everyday connections, across generations, institutions, and the town, are essential when it comes to integrating the Alzheimer’s Village into the local environment and enhancing the sense of continuity and cohesion across different life patterns. The architecture of Alzheimer’s Village caters for the needs of both communities and individuals, providing each resident with options that are reassuring and diverse. Social interactions with other people is often stimulating for the elderly and people with dementia, but being able to choose the time and content of the social activities are essential to ensure a positive experience.
The complex is integrated in nature, transforming the existing landscape with its characteristic ancient pine trees to a recreational space, where residents can relax or go for a walk within the area. A path runs through the landscape, drawing its own loop, so none of the residents will experience dead ends or get lost along the way.
Nature is also in close proximity to the residential units with site-specific grasses, climbing plants and fragrant flowers, which have a stimulating effect and add to the recreational spaces. Espaliers shield and divide the outdoor spaces, giving them a private garden feel, in contrast to the wilder nature near the lake. The complex is grouped into four clusters, each housing around 30 residents who live in smaller ‘families’ with all the necessary facilities and outdoor spaces. Meanwhile, the restaurant, hairdresser’s and research facilities are located together near the main square.

II. Spatial Relationship Descriptions
- Public-to-private hierarchy: a formal, highly public plaza sits at the compositional center and is fringed by communal facilities; housing clusters are positioned around this hub but set back into greener, quieter zones, creating layered degrees of privacy.       
- Adjacency of services and community uses: dining (brasserie), cultural/assembly spaces (auditorium, library) and medical facilities are grouped along the main plaza for shared access and easy wayfinding.
- Clustered residential layout: multiple housing blocks are arranged in loose clusters (L- and U-shaped plans) with small courtyards and terraces that create semi-private outdoor spaces for each cluster.
- Landscape as connective tissue: a network of curving secondary paths, tree canopies and a pond links residential clusters to the central plaza and to support buildings, forming continuous green corridors and visual connections.
- Circulation hierarchy: a strong east–west/main promenade axis and several secondary paths organize pedestrian flow; internal corridors within buildings connect units to shared cores and to the external network.
- Functional zoning: service/technical buildings are located at the periphery (upper-right), separated from residential clusters to minimize disturbance while remaining accessible by service routes.
- Clustering for shared use: communal functions (dining, cultural rooms, medical suite) are physically clustered to be easily shared by multiple residential blocks, reducing duplication and concentrating social life.
- Framing and enclosure: buildings along the plaza form a loose enclosure that defines the public square while remaining porous with multiple entry points and visual permeability to the landscape.

III. Overall Spatial Impression (1–2 sentences)
The scheme reads as a small campus or care-community arranged around a central civic plaza, with residential clusters embedded in a layered landscape that promotes walking, social interaction and calm. Spatial organization emphasizes clear functional zoning—public communal facilities concentrated at the center, private living groups set within quieter green courtyards, and service support at the edges.

IV. Design Intent Reading
- Promote social interaction and community life by concentrating shared amenities and public space at a central focal point that is easily accessible from all housing clusters.
- Create a graduated sequence of spaces (public plaza → semi‑public courtyards → private units) to balance collective activity with privacy and calm for residents.
- Integrate landscape as the primary organizing device to connect buildings, soften scale, and provide therapeutic outdoor spaces (pond, tree canopy, shaded seating).
- Increase operational efficiency and clarity by clustering complementary support functions (dining, medical, cultural) near the plaza while isolating technical/service areas at the periphery.
- Encourage pedestrian movement and legibility through a clear hierarchy of paths and framed sightlines, producing a walkable, human-scaled campus environment.
