Text description provided by the architects. Formerly a railway enclave, the Clichy-Batignolles ecodistrict is reconquering this forgotten piece of Parisian ground. This major municipal project was envisioned as a response to the elevated need for housing while paving the way for a durable, mixed-use 21st century city. So much data that had to be compiled to come up with smart solutions for a multi-program block (nursing home, social housing, private housing, religious center, and retail businesses).
These programs, with their fruitful cohabitation, contribute to the city’s growth with their high quality and symbolic significance. The project provides a strong architectural response to the challenges of urban density and new environmental requirements by creating collective strategies for the entire block. 
The nursing home is located at the very center of the block, which allows its residents to live in the heart of the “city” and benefit from its vitality. The “spiny” facade faces multiple directions and has many diagonal views while nevertheless maintaining a sense of intimacy to the spaces. 
Far from the traditional, old-fashioned setup for senior housing complexes, this project was designed as a whole: as a building, but also in terms of its interior and furniture. 
The rooms, whether they face the city or the patio, all possess an outdoor space and the top floor hosts bedrooms with high confort. The cornerstone of everyday life is the home’s restaurant with a strategic position attuned to the space and pace of the city, two vital functions.

II. Spatial Relationship Descriptions
- The building sits on a triangular lot with its long edge facing the adjacent park; a green berm/landscaped wedge separates and mediates the park and the building.
- A primary entrance and public approach align with Rue René Blum; social and public functions (lobby, open lounge) are placed near this frontage and the entry zone.
- Private units/rooms are organized in a linear sequence along the north and southeastern perimeters, forming double- or single-loaded corridors that face outward for light and views.
- An internal planted courtyard/atrium is carved into the plan and surrounded by communal spaces, creating a semi-private outdoor room that many interior spaces face or open onto.
- Circulation is a combination of curving internal paths (linking social spaces and the courtyard) and straighter corridors that serve the rows of private rooms; vertical cores are centrally located to serve both wings.
- Program is layered from public to private: public approach (street) → lobby/social areas → semi-private courtyard/amenities → private units at the perimeter.
- Landscape elements (berms, terraces, planted pockets) are used to interlock exterior and interior spaces — terraces and planted edges visually and physically connect the interior social areas with the park and street.

III. Overall Spatial Impression (1–2 sentences)
The plan emphasizes an inward-looking social heart (courtyard and lounges) while maximizing perimeter access to light and views for repetitive private units. Geometry and landscaping are used to mediate the triangular lot and to sequence movement from public street 
and park into progressively more private communal and residential spaces.

IV. Design Intent Reading
- Respond to site constraints: The angular, folded geometry adapts to the triangular site and adjacent park, using green wedges and terraces to reconcile building mass and open space.
- Promote social life and daylighting: By anchoring communal programs around a planted courtyard and placing living/working units along the perimeter, the design prioritizes daylight, views, and social interaction within a protected interior landscape.
- Provide a graduated public-to-private experience: The sequence of street/park → entrance lobby → semi-public courtyard → private units suggests an intention to layer privacy and encourage community while maintaining clear access and circulation.
- Integrate landscape as infrastructure: Landscaped berms and terraces act both as buffers and active programmatic spaces, indicating a strategy to blur landscape and architecture for environmental comfort and amenity.
