Text description provided by the architects. In the village of Sint-Ulriks-Kapelle in the heart of Pajottenland in Belgium lies a former castle domain where "De Verlosser" is now an elderly care center. The park domain is a protected heritage site and includes, in addition to the manor house, carriage houses, a farm, a chaplain’s house, etc. To be able to continue to offer high-quality care for the elderly in the future, PLUSOFFICE has made a master plan that strengthens the classified castle and park domain and sets out the guidelines for the further expansion of a residential care center with 150 beds.
As part of this masterplan PLUSOFFICE renovated "Gaudium". This former coach house was used as a novices' residence. The historic facades are restored, the annexes replaced and the design is based on a nearly energy-neutral technology concept. The re-adapted residence will accommodate nine residents, each of whom will have a fully equipped care room and share some special living areas.
The project was designed to first serve as a living community for the sisters including its house chapel, and then later serve as a small-scale residential care home without major modifications. The direct link between the internal residential care community with the castle park and the vegetable garden means the outdoor space can also be actively used as part of the care offer.
PLUSOFFICE's intervention revalues the original main volume, renews its roof shape, and places a "twin-sister" as an extension next to the original building. The new roof form blends seamlessly with the historic building and is made of clay roof tiles in harmony with the existing brick façade. The annex replicates this use of materials and extends it with contemporary window openings. The balustrades in front of these windows are figuratively detailed inspired by the fine wooden cut-outs of the main building.

II. Spatial Relationship Descriptions
- The plan is organized along an east–west axis with rooms laid out in a linear sequence facing a large south-facing terrace/paved forecourt, creating a clear interior–exterior relationship.
- Public functions (living, dining, kitchen) are grouped toward the middle and right (east) and open strongly to the main terrace, encouraging indoor–outdoor flow and social use.
- Private functions (bedrooms and bathrooms) cluster toward the rear (north) in a compact band, sharing sanitary cores and maintaining separation from the public zones.
- A small central atrium/lightwell sits between main rooms, acting as a vertical/visual node that brings light and plantings into the plan and mediates circulation.
- The left wing reads as a more enclosed, possibly historic masonry volume (formal dining/hall) connected to the main sequence via doorways — a cluster that contrasts with the more open, decked zones.
- Thick existing masonry walls and projecting turret elements create strong, solid separators that punctuate and organize the plan while openings and decks create layered transitional spaces between solid and void.
- Circulation is axial and sequential: entry/forecourt → series of interior rooms → terraces; doors align to maintain visual lines and direct movement across spaces.

III. Overall Spatial Impression (1–2 sentences)
The drawing presents a linear, layered arrangement that separates public and private functions while privileging a strong south-facing connection to exterior terraces. Heavy, historic masonry volumes are punctuated by lighter decked spaces and an internal atrium, producing a rhythm of enclosed and open zones.

IV. Design Intent Reading
- Emphasize continuity between interior and exterior: large terraces, decking, and generous openings indicate an intent to blur inside/outside boundaries and to orient primary living areas toward outdoor social space and views.
- Respect and integrate existing historic fabric: the use of thick masonry walls and corner turrets as organizational anchors suggests refurbishment or insertion within an existing structure rather than a wholly new free plan.
- Functional zoning and clarity: the layout deliberately clusters public, private, and service spaces to optimize privacy and flow—public rooms orient to the terrace, bedrooms are kept together at the back, and service/utility elements are compacted.
- Introduce daylighting and ventilation strategies: the central atrium/lightwell and multiple external terraces act as light and air funnels, improving environmental comfort and creating intimate planted pockets within the plan.
- Create a rhythmic sequence of spaces: alternating solid masonry rooms and lighter decked/patio areas produces a measured procession and a layered spatial experience for occupants.
